Science desk   /   Under the hood

How the machine actually works

Six steps inside the Transformer, told with one short sentence: “The bat flew.”

  1. A computer can’t read letters, so each word is swapped for a long list of numbers: its code. Words that mean similar things end up with similar codes. All the words are read at the same time, so each one also gets a seat number, mixed right into its code, to keep the order. Grown-up words: embeddings and positional encoding.

  2. To work out which kind of bat this is, “bat” asks a question. Every word holds up a name tag, and the machine checks how well each tag matches the question. The match scores become shares that add up to 100%. Grown-up words: queries, keys and attention weights (scaled dot-product attention).

  3. Every word also has something to share. “Bat” mixes in a little from each word, in the amounts on the match meter: lots of “flew,” some of itself, a dash of “The.” Its code now leans toward the animal with wings. Grown-up words: a weighted sum of values.

  4. One looker might check who is doing what; another might watch the words next door. The machine runs eight lookers side by side, each with its own questions and name tags, then glues their notes together. Grown-up words: multi-head attention, with 8 heads.

  5. Listening once isn’t enough, so the reader repeats the routine on six floors. On every floor, each word listens to the others, then thinks on its own for a moment. The writer has six floors of its own. Grown-up words: a stack of 6 layers, each with self-attention and a feed-forward network.

  6. The writer builds the German sentence one word at a time. It can read the whole English sentence and the words it has already written, but a cover hides the words still to come. That way it learns to guess the next word fairly. Grown-up words: masked self-attention in the decoder.
